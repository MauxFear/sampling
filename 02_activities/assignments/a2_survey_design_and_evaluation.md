```
For the former employees, we will attempt to reach the entire population given the limited

Observational unit:
```

## Part B - Survey Evaluation:

Identify and describe survey features:

```
1. Sample type:
The study used a two-stage sampling design. The sampling type used was stratified random 
sampling with an additional rejective sampling approach. The strata was defined using 
the geographic areas of the different provinces in Canada by considering Census 
Metropolitan Areas (CMAs). A total of 27 strata were defined. 
The rejective sampling approach was applied by using two versions of the interview, a 
long version and a short version. The long version was sent it to all the volunteers. 
The not volunteers population was splitted randomly into two groups one do the long 
version and the other one the short version of the interview. 

2. Sample size:
The initial sample size was 50,000 units (telephone numbers associated to a household) of
which 40,000 invitation letters to the electronic questionaire were sent to selected 
households across Canada. 

3. Target population:
The target population includes all persons 15 years of age and older living in the ten 
provinces of Canada. It exclude full-time (residing for more than six months) residents 
of institutions. 

4. Sampling frame:
The survey uses a combined sampling frame built from landline and cell phone numbers, 
linked to addresses using Census data, administrative records, and Statistics Canada's dwelling frame. 

5. Survey mode(s): 
The data were collected directly from survey respondents either through an electronic 
questionarie or through CATI (Computer Assisted Telephone interviewing). No proxy 
reporting is allowed. 

6. Timeline:
The data collection period comprised from 2018-09-04 to 2018-12-28.

7. Response rate:
The overall reponse rate was 41.9%

8. Weights: 
In this case the weight was used as the number of persons represented by a given 
respondent. These weights were adjusted to account for the sub-sampling of non-volunteers
(the "rejective" part) and then further calibrated to ensure the survey's income 
distribution accurately matched known provincial totals from the 2017 Canadian Income 
Survey.

9. Data processing:
Data processing utilized Statistics Canada's Social Survey Processing Environment (SSPE). 
It involved automated and manual edits at both macro and micro levels during and after 
collection. These edits included checks for family relationships, data consistency 
(e.g., age vs. birth date), and proper questionnaire flow. Edits were programmed into the 
CATI system for immediate checks, with unresolved issues flagged for head office 
resolution.

10. Cleaning, imputation, etc
Error detection occurred via built-in CATI edits (checking value ranges and questionnaire 
flow) and further detailed edits at the head office. For missing data (item or partial 
non-response), imputation was primarily done using a "nearest neighbour" donor 
method based on a score function comparing respondent characteristics. In cases where 
donor imputation wasn't suitable, mean imputation among a pool of donors was used. Income
data, specifically, was obtained by linking to 2017 tax records (T1FF) for ~82% of 
respondents, with imputation used for the remainder.

11. Sources of error:
- Sampling errors: As the data comes from a sample, estimates may differ from those of 
a full census. Bootstrap weights were created to estimate sampling variability.
- Non-sampling error: Several types of error can be included in this survey such as:   
A coverage error rising from differences between the target popilation and the sampling 
frame, for exampple, households without phone were excluded. Non-response error occurs 
when selected households or individuals do not participate. Weights were adjusted to 
mitigate this. Also, inaccuracies in answers provided by respondents can be a source 
of response errors. Finally, It can be present mistakes made during data handling and 
processing.

12. Limitations, known biases, etc
The survey results might be affected by a few factors. Since it only included households 
with telephones, it could miss perspectives from those without phones (coverage bias). 
There's also a chance that the people who chose not to respond were different from 
those who did (non-response bias), although statistical weights were used to help 
correct for both of these issues. Because the information comes from people recalling 
their activities over the past year, answers could be influenced by memory lapses or 
the desire to give socially acceptable responses. Finally, while linking to tax records 
made the income data more accurate for most, around 18% of incomes had to be estimated, 
which could impact analyses that require very precise income figures.
```
